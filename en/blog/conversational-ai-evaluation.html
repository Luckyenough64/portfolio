Conversational AI / quality metrics / support analytics

Why evaluating Conversational AI is harder than it looks

On quality criteria, and why correctness, usefulness, and satisfaction describe different properties of an answer.

4 quality dimensions2 evaluation layers1 shared context

The central question in chatbot evaluation

It is tempting to ask whether a chatbot gives good answers. That question is too broad to measure directly.

Does “good” mean factually correct, complete, clearly worded, useful for the user's task, or satisfying in the moment? These properties overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

Evaluating a conversational system is a measurement-design problem.

When users like an incorrect answer

A confident, concise response can receive positive feedback even when it contains a factual error or omits an important condition. The user may not have enough information to verify it, or may simply appreciate the tone and speed.

User satisfaction is useful evidence about the experience. It is not a direct substitute for correctness.

For support scenarios, this distinction matters because a pleasant but wrong answer can create delayed costs elsewhere in the process.

When a correct answer causes dissatisfaction

The reverse also occurs. A response may be accurate but long, formal, poorly timed, or unable to complete the action the user expected. Negative feedback then describes a real failure, but not a factual one.

This is why labels should preserve the reason for an assessment instead of compressing every outcome into a single score.

Why one metric is not enough

dimension

Correctness

Are the claims consistent with policies, data, and the known context?

dimension

Completeness

Does the answer include the conditions needed to act safely?

dimension

Expression

Is it understandable, concise, and appropriate for the conversation?

dimension

Usefulness

Does it help the user complete the task or reach the right next step?

A composite score can summarize these dimensions, but the components are still needed for diagnosis.

Limits of automated evaluation

LLM-based judges make large-scale review practical, but they introduce their own sensitivity to prompts, reference answers, context windows, and model versions.

  • the rubric may be underspecified;
  • the judge may prefer a certain writing style;
  • missing conversation context changes the apparent answer;
  • small prompt changes can move category boundaries.

I treat automated labels as a reproducible analytical layer, then calibrate them against reviewed examples and disagreement analysis.

The risk of optimizing a single metric

Optimizing only satisfaction may reward confident shortcuts. Optimizing only factual correctness may produce defensive, unhelpful responses. Optimizing containment may discourage necessary escalation.

A metric becomes dangerous when the team starts treating its proxy as the full objective.

The role of human handoff

Support quality includes handing a conversation to an agent when the automated path does not cover the situation.

Escalation is not automatically a chatbot failure. It can be the correct behavior under uncertainty, policy constraints, or requests that require access the model does not have.

Comparison with support evaluation

Traditional support analytics already separates resolution, satisfaction, compliance, handling time, and repeat contact. Conversational AI needs the same discipline, plus controls for generated content and evaluation reproducibility.

The useful unit of analysis is often not a single message but the full conversation and the outcome that followed.

What these studies taught me

01

There is no single quality metric

Different metrics describe different failure modes.

02

Satisfaction and quality are not the same

They should be analyzed together, not substituted for one another.

03

Automated evaluation has limits

It needs calibration, versioning, and reviewed examples.

04

Quality starts with explicit criteria

A stable rubric is more valuable than a sophisticated score with unclear meaning.

05

A good system knows its boundaries

Reliable escalation is part of the product behavior.

Conclusion

Conversational AI evaluation is not a ranking exercise with one universal number. It is a set of linked measurements that need context, definitions, and known limitations.

The aim is not to remove judgment from the process, but to make that judgment explicit and reproducible.

Conversational AI evaluation combines analytics, labeling, and contextual research.
