Analytical systems / metadata / data modeling

What three billion partitions taught me about analytical systems

On metadata costs, granularity choices, and the limits of scaling with resources.

3B partitions104 nodes29M per node

Sometimes data volume is not the problem

Over several years of working with analytical infrastructure, I have seen heavy queries, unfortunate joins, memory exhaustion, and uneven data distribution. One case stood out.

During a technical discussion, we examined a schema with very fine partitioning. On a 104-node cluster, the total had grown to roughly three billion partitions, or almost 29 million per node.

At that scale, the issue is no longer just storing data. Metadata and maintenance operations become part of the system's primary workload. The case showed how the cost of a data-model decision accumulates as a platform grows.

Why partitioning looks attractive

Partitioning is a common optimization in analytical systems. Time-based data can be split by month, week, or day so that a query scans only the relevant sections.

  • less data scanned;
  • faster deletion of historical ranges;
  • more predictable maintenance;
  • better parallelism in some workloads.

The trade-off begins when granularity is chosen for a local use case without estimating its platform-wide cost.

Partitions are more than data

Every partition carries metadata: identifiers, boundaries, statistics, file references, ownership, and state. The system has to load, update, synchronize, and inspect that metadata.

A small unit repeated billions of times is no longer small. Even compact metadata structures become a material memory and CPU workload.

The relevant question is therefore not only how much user data a partition contains, but how many objects the control plane must manage.

Resources do not compensate for the data model

Adding nodes distributes user data, but it also distributes the burden of metadata management. It may postpone the threshold without changing the underlying growth pattern.

In this case, each node still had to operate with tens of millions of partition records. More machines changed the denominator, not the nature of the workload.

Metadata can become the bottleneck

Metadata costs can grow faster than a data-volume estimate alone would suggest.

Planning, catalog access, recovery, statistics updates, and background maintenance all touch metadata. A query may read little data and still spend meaningful time deciding what to read.

What many small partitions change

  • query planning becomes more expensive;
  • catalog and memory pressure increase;
  • maintenance operations touch more objects;
  • recovery and synchronization take longer;
  • operational tooling has to process much larger inventories.

None of these effects is surprising in isolation. Their combined cost is easy to underestimate during initial schema design.

The same pattern appears elsewhere

I have seen similar behavior with tiny files in HDFS, excessive table fragments, over-segmented event storage, and intermediate datasets split into thousands of outputs. The technologies differ, but the pattern is stable: object count becomes an independent scaling dimension.

Why platforms allow such schemas

A database cannot always distinguish a deliberate high-granularity design from a harmful one. Hard limits would block valid workloads, so many systems expose the mechanism and leave the cost model to the team operating it.

This makes capacity reviews and representative load tests part of data modeling, not a separate operational concern.

What I took from the case

01

Metadata has a cost

Object count belongs in capacity estimates alongside bytes and rows.

02

Scaling does not fix architectural constraints

Additional nodes can delay a limit while preserving the same growth curve.

03

Every optimization has a price

Faster pruning may mean more metadata and more expensive maintenance.

04

A sound data model pays off for years

Granularity decisions are repeated by every query and operational process that follows.

Conclusion

The central lesson was not that partitioning is bad. It was that its useful unit depends on the scale and behavior of the whole system.

A design that is inexpensive at thousands of objects may have a different profile at billions. Bytes alone do not describe that transition.

Data-model decisions shape the workload profile before infrastructure begins to scale.
