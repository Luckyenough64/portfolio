Experiments / product analytics / statistical reasoning

When A/B test results mislead

Why statistical significance alone does not determine a product decision.

α error controlMDE sensitivityΔ practical effect

Interpretation changes the result

An A/B test does not literally lie. It answers a statistical question under a set of assumptions. Trouble begins when that answer is translated into a broader product claim.

A significant difference can be too small to matter. A non-significant result can be underpowered. A positive aggregate can hide opposite effects across segments.

A meaningful share of disputed conclusions appears during interpretation.

What an A/B test actually tells us

Under the chosen design, sample, metric, and model, the observed data are more or less compatible with the null hypothesis. That is narrower than “the feature works” or “nothing happened.”

The result is conditional on assignment quality, metric definitions, exposure, sample size, and the analysis plan.

The “nothing happened” case

A team once read a non-significant result as evidence that a change had no effect. The confidence interval, however, still included both a useful improvement and a meaningful decline.

No significance does not mean no effect.

The experiment had reduced uncertainty, but not enough to support the categorical conclusion being made.

Why MDE belongs before launch

Minimum detectable effect connects sample size with the smallest effect the experiment is designed to identify. Without it, teams may run a test that cannot answer the decision they care about.

  • define the decision-relevant effect;
  • estimate baseline variance and traffic;
  • select power and error thresholds;
  • calculate the required duration before exposure begins.

MDE is not a promise that the effect will exist. It defines the sensitivity of the design.

When aggregates hide segment differences

An overall uplift can be driven by one high-volume group while another group deteriorates. The reverse is also possible: stable aggregate performance can conceal large offsetting changes.

Segment analysis should be planned carefully because unconstrained slicing creates multiple-testing and storytelling risks. The goal is to examine plausible mechanisms, not to search until a preferred pattern appears.

Statistical and practical significance

Large samples can make very small differences statistically significant. A decision still needs the absolute effect, implementation cost, risk, reversibility, and distribution across users.

Statistical significance does not guarantee business significance.

Peeking at interim results

Repeatedly checking a conventional fixed-horizon test and stopping when the p-value crosses a threshold changes its error properties.

If sequential decisions are required, use a design that accounts for them and define stopping rules in advance.

The operational convenience of a dashboard does not change the assumptions of the statistical procedure behind it.

When there are too many metrics

With enough metrics, segments, and time windows, some differences will appear by chance. A useful experiment separates a primary outcome, guardrails, and diagnostic metrics before results are visible.

Secondary metrics remain valuable, but their role in the decision should be explicit.

Why A/B tests remain useful

None of these limitations makes controlled experiments less valuable. Randomization is still a strong way to separate treatment effects from many confounders.

The discipline comes from matching the claim to the design: what was randomized, who was exposed, which outcome was measured, and what uncertainty remains.

What experiments taught me

01

No significance does not mean no effect

Read the interval and the design's sensitivity.

02

Significance does not mean business value

Translate relative changes into absolute outcomes and costs.

03

Aggregates can hide important differences

Use planned segment analysis to examine mechanisms.

04

A good experiment starts before launch

Define metrics, MDE, duration, and stopping rules in advance.

05

The test does not decide what to do

It provides evidence for a decision with product constraints.

Conclusion

An experiment is most useful when it narrows a specific uncertainty. The strength of the conclusion cannot exceed the sensitivity and assumptions of the design.

Good analysis reports the effect, its uncertainty, relevant heterogeneity, and the limits of the data.

The purpose of an experiment is to reduce uncertainty as far as its design and data allow.
